# The Utilitarian Failure of Mandatory Real-Name Systems


## Reflection on Group Presentation


In our group presentation regarding the "Real-Name System (RNS) on the Internet," my role was to present the neutral perspective and the final conclusion. We argued that RNS poses threats to privacy and autonomy. However, upon reflecting on the feedback received, I realized a critical deficiency in our analysis: while we utilized ethical theories to construct abstract arguments, we lacked sufficient empirical evidence to substantiate our claims. Specifically, we did not provide concrete historical examples to prove that the costs of RNS outweigh its benefits. This report aims to bridge this gap by analyzing RNS through the lens of Utilitarianism, supported by the specific case study of South Korea.


## Utilitarianism and Empirical Evidence


Utilitarianism posits that an action is morally right if it maximizes overall happiness and minimizes suffering for the greatest number of people (Mill, 1863). Proponents of RNS often argue from a utilitarian standpoint, claiming that the sacrifice of privacy is a necessary pain to reduce cyberbullying and foster the pleasure of an orderly society. However, when we apply actual evidence to this happiness calculus, the argument for RNS collapses.


## Data Security and The South Korean Failure


The most compelling evidence against RNS comes from South Korea, the first country to implement a mandatory "Internet Real-Name System" in 2007. From a utilitarian perspective, the policy resulted in massive social harm, or pain, that far exceeded any benefits. In 2011, the "Nate" hacking incident led to the leakage of personal data of 35 million users, representing approximately 70% of the country's population at the time(Park & Greenleaf, 2012). This catastrophic breach demonstrated that centralized identification databases create a single point of failure, exposing citizens to fraud and identity theft. The immense psychological distress and financial risk imposed on millions of citizens constitute a significant negative utility.


## Ineffectiveness and the Chilling Effect


Furthermore, utilitarianism requires the measure to be effective in producing the intended pleasure of reducing toxicity. Evidence shows RNS failed in this regard. Studies following the Korean implementation indicated that malicious comments decreased by only 0.9%, a statistically insignificant margin, while overall user participation dropped significantly due to the chilling effect (Cho, 2011). This validates the argument I raised in the presentation regarding marginalized groups: RNS silences whistleblowers and minorities who rely on anonymity for safety. The suppression of free speech and the lack of safety improvement led the Constitutional Court of Korea to rule the RNS unconstitutional in 2012, citing that the public interest did not justify the restriction on freedom (Park & Greenleaf, 2012).


## Conclusion


In conclusion, applying Utilitarianism with historical evidence reveals that mandatory RNS is unethical. The specific case of South Korea proves that such systems generate disproportionate harm through data breaches and suppression of speech, without delivering the promised benefit of a cleaner internet. Therefore, as suggested in my presentation's conclusion, "Accountable Anonymity"—rather than mandatory identification—is the ethically superior approach to balance order and liberty.
